\section{Domain-Specific Offline Multi-Object Tracking}
\label{sec:tracking}

The tracker is organized around the asymmetric error cost introduced in
\cref{sec:problem}.  The production pipeline therefore separates association into
two levels:
\begin{equation}
  \text{detections}
  \longrightarrow \text{high-purity local tracklets}
  \longrightarrow \text{globally optimized trajectories}.
  \label{eq:tracking-two-stage}
\end{equation}
The first stage resolves only short, unambiguous transitions.  The second can inspect
all tracklets in the recording before deciding which gaps to bridge.

\subsection{Why general-purpose components were not sufficient}

The project investigated OC-SORT~\cite{ocsort} and BoT-SORT~\cite{botsort}, including
variants with camera-motion compensation and appearance features.  It also implemented appearance backends based
on OSNet person re-identification and a general Vision Transformer.  These are useful
reference designs, but their usual operating assumptions do not match this narrow
application particularly well.  A causal tracker must commit while two sails overlap
or cross; subsequent frames may reveal the correct continuation, but arrive too late
to prevent the original identity switch.  Moreover, a person-ReID network devotes its
capacity to cues such as clothing and body appearance.  In long-shot windsurfing
footage, the person occupies very few pixels, whereas the sail is large, deliberately
colorful, and often visually distinctive.

This experience does not imply that the custom tracker is generally superior to
OC-SORT, BoT-SORT, or learned ReID.  The comparison is specific to shore-recorded
windsurfing, offline processing, and a loss function dominated by even a single false
merge.  The implemented comparison and optimization tools document that the
alternatives were implemented and explored, but the current benchmark harness has drifted from the
production embedding and tracker interfaces.  We therefore avoid a numerical
outperformance claim until the baselines have been rerun on aligned inputs and
representative manually verified sequences.

The design record can be summarized as follows. These are observations from project
footage, not a controlled leaderboard.

\subsection{Sail color as an appearance descriptor}

The production appearance backend replaces generic semantic features with a compact
descriptor tailored to the most stable visible property in this domain: sail color.
For every detection crop it computes a joint histogram of the chromatic Lab channels
$(a,b)$, a circular hue histogram, and a lower-weight luminance histogram.  Pixel
contributions are weighted by $S^\gamma$, where $S$ is HSV saturation, so strongly
colored sail panels contribute more than grey water, spray, or haze.  Hue votes are
linearly shared between adjacent circular bins, avoiding a discontinuity at the hue
wraparound.

Color alone would discard useful coarse layout.  The crop is consequently divided
into three bands along its vertical axis, with a fourth block covering the complete
crop.  The Lab, hue, and luminance histograms are concatenated for each block.  Every
histogram first receives an L1 normalization and square-root mapping; the concatenated
vector is then L2-normalized.  The resulting representation supports a bounded
Hellinger-style distance through the cosine similarity of the transformed vectors.
Tracklet appearance prototypes are exponential moving averages, which reduce the
effect of individual blurred, partially occluded, or poorly exposed crops.

The offline linker does not treat raw cosine overlap as a match probability.  Because the
descriptor consists of non-negative histogram features, two different sails can
still share substantial histogram support, including residual water and background;
the displayed example has a mean cross-sail cosine overlap of approximately 0.50.
Production association maps the corresponding distance $d=1-\operatorname{cos}$ to
the bounded heuristic score
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathrm{appearance}}
  = \operatorname{clip}_{[10^{-6},\,1-10^{-6}]}(1-11.630d).
  \label{eq:tracking-appearance-score}
\end{equation}
This deliberately turns only very small descriptor distances into strong candidate
evidence; it is a fitted association score, not a calibrated probability.

The descriptor also records a useful negative result.  An earlier implementation
estimated foreground from the color statistics of the crop border, using a robust Lab
background model, a smoothness gate, and connected components.  In the active path
this is bypassed by a simple mask that removes the outer 10\% of the crop.  The fixed
centre margin is less ambitious, but removes the descriptor's dependence on a
background model as the scene changes from beach to water or a sail touches the
bounding-box edge.  This is an example of domain adaptation through simplification:
the descriptor need not segment the complete rig if its central, saturated color
distribution is already sufficiently discriminative.

\subsection{Conservative formation of local tracklets}

Each detection initially forms a one-observation track.  A greedy preprocessor then
processes frames in temporal order and maintains, for every active tracklet, a
constant-velocity Kalman state and an exponentially averaged appearance descriptor.
Before scoring a detection, the Kalman state is advanced through the measured
frame-to-frame camera transforms.  Thus the expected box centre follows both the
surfer's estimated velocity and the image displacement induced by camera pan and
rotation.

Candidate transitions are gated by two probabilities: one derived from the
position-space Mahalanobis distance of the camera-compensated prediction, and one
derived from sail-appearance distance.  Strict matches require both cues to pass high
thresholds and may span at most three frames. Looser candidates can remain eligible
for up to six frames when supported by one strong cue or both weaker cues. Under the
clean-first rule, one strict candidate wins even if loose alternatives exist; with no
strict candidate, exactly one loose candidate may win. Multiple strict candidates,
multiple loose-only candidates, duplicate proposals to one tracklet, or overlap with
a newly created track preserve the ambiguity as separate tracklets.
This deliberate strategy trades recall for purity: uncertainty is preserved for the
offline stage instead of being hidden inside an irreversible local decision.

\subsection{Camera-compensated candidate costs}

Let $T_i$ and $T_j$ be non-overlapping tracklets with $T_i$ ending before $T_j$
begins.  The offline tracker creates a directed candidate edge $i\rightarrow j$ only
when the temporal gap is non-negative and no longer than the configured horizon,
currently three seconds.  A Kalman filter is fitted once to all detections of $T_i$.
Its end state is then advanced to the first observations of $T_j$, applying the
estimated camera transform at every intervening frame.  This matters during long
pans: without compensation, the shared movement of the entire image would dominate
the residual motion that distinguishes one surfer from another.

Every candidate receives a weighted negative-log score
\begin{equation}
  C_{ij} = w_m C^{\mathrm{motion}}_{ij}
         + w_a C^{\mathrm{appearance}}_{ij}
         + w_g C^{\mathrm{gap}}_{ij}.
  \label{eq:tracking-edge-cost}
\end{equation}
The motion term averages $-\log p$ scores obtained from the Mahalanobis distance to
the first two detections of $T_j$.  The appearance term compares an exponentially
weighted prototype at the end of $T_i$ with a reverse-weighted prototype at the
beginning of $T_j$ and maps the Hellinger-style distance to a heuristic match score.  The
gap term is
\begin{equation}
  C^{\mathrm{gap}}_{ij} = -\Delta f_{ij}\log p_{\mathrm{miss}},
  \label{eq:tracking-gap-cost}
\end{equation}
so a proposed link must accumulate stronger motion and appearance evidence as the
number $\Delta f_{ij}$ of unobserved frames grows.  The fitted weights calibrate the
relative influence of these three terms for the project data; they should not be
interpreted as universal MOT constants.

The three-second horizon is a deliberate safety bound, not a claim that arbitrary
occlusions can be recovered. Prolonged co-visibility and partial overlap can last much
longer because detections remain available; a complete missing-observation gap beyond
the horizon remains fragmented by design.

\subsection{Global optimization over a bounded candidate graph}

The candidate graph is solved as a minimum-cost integer program.  For each tracklet
$i$, binary variables represent starting a trajectory at $i$, ending at $i$, linking
$i$ to a candidate successor, or---for eligible short fragments---discarding $i$.
The constraints require exactly one incoming choice and exactly one outgoing choice
for every retained tracklet.  The selected edges therefore form disjoint,
time-ordered paths rather than competing local assignments.

Starting and ending are explicit alternatives to linking.  Their costs are reduced
when a tracklet begins or ends within the outer 10\% of the image, because a surfer
entering or leaving the field of view is more plausible there than in the centre.
An edge is removed before optimization whenever linking $i$ to $j$ is no cheaper than
ending $i$ and starting $j$ independently.  The graph is further limited to the best
few outgoing candidates per node.  These operations reduce solver work while
preserving the intended decision boundary: a weak link should become a fragmentation,
not an identity switch.

Very short detector artifacts pose a separate problem.  For fragments up to a
configured number of detections, the optimizer exposes a discard option whose cost grows
exponentially with fragment length.  A one-frame false positive can therefore be
removed without forcing it to become a trajectory or attach to a real surfer, while
discarding a sustained observation rapidly becomes unattractive and is eventually
disallowed.

The advantage of this formulation is non-causal rather than mystical ``future
knowledge.''  A difficult edge is judged using a stable tracklet before the gap, a
stable tracklet after it, and the other feasible links throughout the recording.
The integer program selects these alternatives jointly, so two locally plausible
continuations cannot both claim the same successor.  The current production pipeline
uses this single-pass \texttt{ILPTracker}; the older iterative ILP implementation
remains an experiment and is not the authoritative execution path.

\subsection{From linked tracklets to renderable trajectories}

The selected paths still contain sparse detections. A final post-processor removes
tracks that are too short or too sparsely observed, then runs a camera-compensated
forward Kalman pass and backward Rauch--Tung--Striebel smoother. It fills internal
gaps with interpolated boxes and attributes before assigning compact output labels.
The result is a dense, frame-indexed trajectory that the pose stage can turn into a
stable crop signal without confusing interpolated observations with detector output.
